the security of a cipher must never depend on keeping the algorithm itself a
secret. this principle was stated by auguste kerckhoffs in the nineteenth
century, and it remains the foundation of modern cryptography. a system should
stay secure even when everything about it, except the key, is public knowledge.
substitution ciphers fail this test badly. they hide the identity of each
letter, but they do not hide the shape of the language underneath. the letter e
is the most common letter in english text, and it stays the most common letter
after the substitution is applied. the word the appears more often than any
other word, and a three letter word that repeats constantly is a very strong
clue. frequency analysis exploits exactly this leakage. an analyst counts the
single letters, then the pairs, then the triples, and matches those counts
against the known statistics of the language. within a few careful guesses the
mapping begins to collapse, and once a handful of letters are fixed the
remaining words can be read almost directly. modern block ciphers defeat this
attack because a single change in the input changes roughly half of the output
bits, and because the same plaintext block does not always produce the same
ciphertext block. that last property depends on the mode of operation rather
than on the cipher itself, which is why choosing a mode carelessly can undo all
of the strength that the underlying algorithm provides.
